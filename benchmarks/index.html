Benchmarks

SCS 3.3 is fast on large problems and reliable across problem classes, and a single solver covers linear, quadratic, second-order cone, semidefinite, exponential cone and power cone programs. This page compares it with the leading open-source solvers on standard quadratic program (QP), linear program (LP) and semidefinite program (SDP) test sets, using the open solver_benchmarks harness: every solver gets the same problem in its native form and the same time limit, and every solver, SCS included, has each returned solution checked independently against the problem data. The raw results, the campaign configuration and the scripts that make the figures and tables are in bodono/solver_benchmarks_results (campaign scs332_2026-11), so every number here can be regenerated.

The highlights, at a target accuracy of \(10^{-4}\) (SCS’s default tolerance):

  • Fastest on the largest problems. With the cuDSS GPU backend, SCS has the lowest geometric mean solve time of any solver tested on the largest quarter of the QPs and on the largest quarter of the LPs, and it verifies all 40 of the largest QPs.

  • Large LPs. On Hans Mittelmann’s set of large LPs, SCS with cuDSS verifies 29 of the 37 instances, more than any other solver, in less than half the geometric mean time of the runner-up, SCS on the CPU. It is the only solver that solves Dual2_5000, the largest instance, with 126 million nonzeros.

  • Ahead of the other first-order and GPU solvers. On every QP and LP set, both SCS backends verify more problems than OSQP, ProxQP, OR-Tools PDLP and NVIDIA cuOpt, in less time.

  • Competitive with interior-point solvers. On the CPU, SCS verifies more of the largest LPs and of the Mittelmann LPs than any interior-point solver, in less time, and over all 349 LPs SCS with cuDSS verifies the most problems of any solver.

  • Semidefinite programs. SCS verifies more of the 94 SDPs than any other solver shown (83 on both backends). On the 31 large sparse relaxations, SCS on the CPU and SDPA each verify 29, while Clarabel and CVXOPT hit memory, array-size or time limits on many of them.

  • Infeasibility. SCS returns certificates of infeasibility or unboundedness that can be checked against the problem data. With cuDSS and a solve tolerance of \(10^{-8}\) it certifies 27 of the 29 infeasible Netlib LPs, tied with Clarabel for the most.

Interior-point solvers are usually faster on small problems and on the SDPs they can solve, and they pull ahead on QPs at tighter accuracy; the sections below show where.

Methodology

Verification. No solver’s “optimal” status is taken at face value. The harness recomputes the relative primal residual, dual residual and duality gap of every returned solution from the problem data, and a solve counts only when all three are below ten times the target accuracy of the plot or table (\(10^{-3}\) for the \(10^{-4}\) target), whatever tolerance the run requested: a reported optimum that fails counts as a failure, and a return reported inaccurate or timed out that passes counts as a success. Solvers define “tolerance” differently, and first-order methods, SCS included, occasionally stop on badly scaled problems where the unscaled residuals are large, so the same independent check for every solver keeps the comparison fair.

Tolerances and the runs shown. Each solver was run at two or three requested tolerances, listed in the table below. SCS used its default settings apart from eps_abs = eps_rel = tol, the time limit and an iteration limit raised to \(10^{6}\); its default tolerance, \(10^{-4}\), is the headline target. A requested tolerance means different things to different solvers, so at each target SCS, OSQP and cuOpt are shown from the run requested at the target, and PDLP and ProxQP from their next tighter run where there is one, which comes closer to the target in achieved accuracy. The interior-point and simplex solvers are not loosened to the target: each is shown from its tightest run, the one nearest its default accuracy (\(10^{-6}\), and Clarabel’s default \(10^{-8}\) on the SDPs; PIQP and HiGHS ran only at \(10^{-4}\) on the Mittelmann set), so their verified solutions are usually far more accurate than the target (second table below). Their own defaults lie between \(10^{-6}\) and \(10^{-9}\), each in its own measure of the residual.

Timing. The reported time is wall-clock time for the solver call, including any presolve, factorization and GPU transfer, but not reading the problem from disk. The time limits are 300 s for QPs and LPs, 900 s for SDPs and 1800 s for the Mittelmann LPs, passed to each solver’s own time-limit setting where it has one (PIQP, ProxQP, SDPA and CVXOPT have none, and the harness stops them); a solve counts only if it finished within the limit plus a 60 s grace. A performance profile shows the fraction of the problems each solver solves within a factor \(\tau\) of the fastest solver on each problem, so the height of a curve at the right edge is the solver’s success rate. The shifted geometric mean uses a shift of 10 s and charges each failure three times the time limit, more than any successful solve costs. Times below 10 ms are floored at 10 ms before computing ratios.

Hardware. Every solve ran on Modal, on one CPU type for the CPU solvers and one GPU type for the GPU solvers, so that no solver’s times depend on which machine a job happened to land on (a container that started on any other hardware handed its job back). The CPU solvers ran in 4-core Linux x86-64 containers on AMD EPYC Zen 3 (“Milan”) hosts, with every threading library limited to 4 threads; SCS used MKL Pardiso, the linear system solver the scs wheel selects by default on Linux (built from the 3.3.2 sources against the same static MKL as the wheel). The two GPU solvers, SCS with cuDSS and NVIDIA cuOpt, ran on an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB with 8 cores of an AMD EPYC Zen 3 host; their times include host-device transfer and cuDSS analysis, so small problems pay a fixed overhead of roughly 0.4 s.

Solvers. SCS 3.3.2 (CPU with MKL Pardiso, and GPU with cuDSS 1.0.0), Clarabel 0.11.1, PIQP 0.6.4, OSQP 1.1.3, ProxQP 0.7.3 (proxsuite), HiGHS 1.15.1, PDLP from OR-Tools 9.15, NVIDIA cuOpt 26.8.0, CVXOPT 1.3.3 and SDPA via sdpa-python 0.2.3. Apart from SCS, each was the latest release on PyPI in September 2026; every solver was run again for this page in November 2026, on the hardware above. On the GPU, SCS ran cuDSS without pivoting, matching or iterative refinement, so that, like the CPU direct solver, it computes a plain LDLT factorization of the quasi-definite KKT matrix; this is the change in cvxgrp/scs#525, which the GPU build includes (see Sparse GPU direct method). Commercial solvers were not included.

Problem sets. QP: Maros-Meszaros (138) and the QPLIB continuous convex subset (19). LP: Netlib (feasible), Kennington and the root LP relaxations of all 240 instances of the MIPLIB 2017 benchmark set; the Mittelmann LP set is its own section. SDP: the 88 feasible SDPLIB instances and the 6 Mittelmann SDPs. The 29 infeasible Netlib LPs and SDPLIB’s four infeasible instances are used only in Infeasible and unbounded problems. “Largest quartile” means the quarter of each family with the most nonzeros in the constraint matrix (plus the Hessian for QPs).

Exclusions. Every failure counts against the solver, whatever its cause. Clarabel exceeded the 64 GB memory limit on equalG11, equalG51, maxG55 and G40mc in the September campaign, so with the same version and memory it was not run on them again; its worker aborted on maxG60. cuOpt’s QP path stopped with a numerical error on 47 of the 157 QPs in every run, and its worker crashed on 14 of the 349 LPs in its \(10^{-4}\) run; PIQP’s worker crashed on the QPLIB instance 9008. All of these count as failures.

Quadratic programs

157 problems: Maros-Meszaros (138) and the convex continuous QPLIB instances (19). On the largest quarter (the top row of the headline figure), SCS with cuDSS has the lowest geometric mean time of any solver and verifies all 40, and SCS on the CPU verifies 37, between Clarabel (38) and PIQP (35), and is faster than PIQP. Both backends verify more than OSQP, ProxQP and cuOpt, in less time. Over all 157 problems, most of them small, the interior-point solvers Clarabel and PIQP verify a few more and are faster on most of the smaller problems, where the GPU backend also pays its fixed overhead of about 0.4 s per solve.

At the tighter \(10^{-6}\) target the interior-point solvers pull ahead on the largest QPs, as expected against a first-order method, but SCS with cuDSS still verifies as many of them as Clarabel (34 of 40), and SCS remains the fastest first-order solver by a wide margin.

Large linear programs: the Mittelmann set

The LP test sets above are dominated by small and medium instances, so we also ran the 37 problems of Hans Mittelmann’s LP benchmark set, the standard collection of large, hard LPs: between 100,000 and 126 million nonzeros, with several instances of 10 to 40 million variables. Every solver ran with an 1800 s limit in 64 GB containers (4 cores, or an A100 80GB for the two GPU solvers) and is shown at the target of \(10^{-4}\) from the run chosen as described under Methodology: Clarabel from its \(10^{-6}\) run, PDLP from its \(10^{-5}\) run and the others from their \(10^{-4}\) run. OSQP was not run on this set. Mittelmann’s own runs allow several hours per instance and use faster machines, so the simplex and interior-point codes time out here far more often than they do in his tables; the point of this set for us is the size of the problems, not a re-run of his benchmark.

This is where the cuDSS backend pays off. SCS with cuDSS verifies 29 of the 37, more than any other solver, with a geometric mean time of 152 s: less than half that of SCS on the CPU, which is second (24 verified, 361 s), and less than a quarter of that of the best of the other solvers, Clarabel (21 verified, 693 s). It is the only solver that solves Dual2_5000, the largest instance, with 126 million nonzeros. OR-Tools PDLP, the other first-order solver run on this set, verifies 19, and cuOpt, although it reports 36 of the 37 instances optimal, verifies 8 (see the notes below).

The ordering is unchanged at the tighter \(10^{-5}\) setting, at which SCS, PDLP and cuOpt were also run on this set:

Infeasible and unbounded problems

A solver should also recognise when a problem has no solution, and SCS says so with a certificate of infeasibility or unboundedness that anyone can check against the problem data. We ran every LP solver on the 29 infeasible LPs of the Netlib collection and every SDP solver on the four infeasible SDPLIB instances (two primal infeasible, two unbounded). These are small problems, a median of 460 variables, so this tests detection rather than speed at scale.

SCS with cuDSS certifies 27 of the 29 LPs, tied with Clarabel for the most, and both SCS backends certify all four SDPs. At a solve tolerance of \(10^{-4}\), SCS on the CPU certifies 23 of the LPs in 0.12 s geometric mean, and most of the rest are near-feasible (defined below); with the solve tolerance tightened to \(10^{-8}\) it certifies 25, and 26 with the solve and certificate tolerances both at \(10^{-6}\). Of the other solvers, HiGHS detects 26 of the LPs by status, OSQP and PDLP give no answer on 10 and 5, and on the SDPs SDPA gets all four by status, while CVXOPT’s certificates fail the check.

SCS, Clarabel, OSQP, PDLP and CVXOPT return a certificate (a Farkas ray), which the harness checks against the problem data at the same \(10^{-3}\) threshold as the plots; HiGHS, PIQP, cuOpt and SDPA report a status only. A verified certificate of either kind counts as correct (cplex1 and mondou2 are both primal and dual infeasible). “Near-feasible” means the solver returned a point whose residuals pass the check: the instance is infeasible by less than the tolerance asked for. The plot shows SCS, Clarabel and OSQP at a solve tolerance of \(10^{-8}\) with the certificate tolerance at \(10^{-4}\) (a tight solve tolerance is what stops a near-feasible point from being accepted as optimal), and PDLP at \(10^{-5}\) with its default certificate tolerance, which verifies more than a loosened one. A verified certificate counts as a solve and everything else as a failure, with the same charge as the other sets. The table shows the same settings plus SCS at \(10^{-4}\); the other runs are in the results repository.

Semidefinite programs

94 problems: the 88 feasible SDPLIB instances and the 6 Mittelmann SDPs. SCS verifies more of them than any other solver shown: 83 on both backends, against 76 for CVXOPT, 75 for SDPA and 71 for Clarabel. On the 31 large sparse combinatorial relaxations (theta, mcp, maxG, qpG, equalG and the Mittelmann G40mc), SCS on the CPU verifies 29, as many as SDPA, and SCS on the GPU 28, while CVXOPT verifies 21 and Clarabel 19, hitting CVXOPT’s INT_MAX array-size limit, the memory limit or the time limit on most of the rest.

Where an interior-point solver does solve an SDP, it is usually faster: the fastest of them beats SCS on the CPU on 73 of the 82 SDPs that SCS on the CPU and at least one of them verify, and SDPA and CVXOPT have the lowest geometric mean times, several times lower than SCS’s on the largest quarter. Most of SCS’s failures are badly conditioned control and gpp instances, the truss design problems buck3 and trto3, and the two largest max-cut relaxations, maxG55 and maxG60 (PSD blocks of order 5000 and 7000), which SDPA does not solve within the limit either. The GPU backend does not help on SDPs: the time goes into the eigendecompositions of the cone projection, not the linear system.

Sensitivity to the requested tolerance

The headline plots target \(10^{-4}\). To show what a tighter target costs, the same three rows are repeated with a target of \(10^{-5}\), every solve verified at \(10^{-4}\), and each solver shown from the run given for that target in the table under Methodology. The interior-point and simplex solvers are shown from the same runs as at \(10^{-4}\), but the tighter check still costs Clarabel and HiGHS some solves.

SCS with cuDSS keeps the lowest geometric mean time on the largest LPs and still leads the Mittelmann set, where SCS on the CPU verifies as many as Clarabel (21) in less time. On the largest QPs SCS with cuDSS still verifies the most (38 of 40, against 37 for Clarabel), but two instances now reach the time limit and Clarabel is slightly faster; PIQP and cuOpt are faster than SCS on the CPU there. On the largest LPs OSQP and cuOpt lose about a third and a quarter of their solves (46 to 32 and 27 to 20), and on the Mittelmann set PDLP loses a quarter of its solves (19 to 14), against at most 14% for SCS on those sets, so SCS’s margin over them widens there. On the largest LPs PDLP loses about as many as SCS (52 to 49), and on the largest QPs OSQP loses only one (32 to 31).

Notes on individual solvers

  • cuOpt. Run with its default LP method and default settings apart from the tolerance and time limit. It reports most LPs optimal (335 of the 349 in its \(10^{-4}\) run), but 105 of those fail the infinity-norm residual check used here, 98 of them Kennington and MIPLIB problems with many variable bounds: its PDLP stops on an L2-relative rule, and on these problems its returned duals often have large infinity-norm stationarity residuals. The LP answers that pass are very accurate (median residual around \(10^{-10}\)), and a tighter request changes little: its \(10^{-4}\), \(10^{-5}\) and \(10^{-6}\) runs verify 230, 232 and 238 of the 349 LPs at \(10^{-3}\). Its QP path is a barrier method whose solutions verify cleanly.

  • PDLP (OR-Tools). The same L2-relative termination rule applies, with a milder effect: at a requested \(10^{-4}\), 28 of its 307 “optimal” LP returns have infinity-norm residuals above \(10^{-3}\). It is shown from its \(10^{-5}\) run, where 2 of 293 do.

  • HiGHS. Its QP solver is an active-set method and is slow on the larger QPs; its LP simplex has the lowest geometric mean time over the full LP set.

  • ProxQP. Run with its default dense/sparse backend selection; it times out on many of the larger problems.

  • Clarabel. Shown from its \(10^{-6}\) run on QP and LP and from its default \(10^{-8}\) run on SDP. Each SDPLIB instance with a PSD block of order 500 or more that it was run on had a 64 GB container of its own.

  • SDPA and CVXOPT. Interior-point SDP solvers, with the lowest geometric mean times on the SDP set. Neither takes a time limit, so the harness stops a solve that runs past it.
